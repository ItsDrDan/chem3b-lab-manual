# Experiment 13: Peptides and Proteins

## 1. Inventory

- **Parts**
  - A: model glycine and serine, build Gly–Ser and Ser–Gly, hydrolyze Ser–Gly.
  - B: class peptide built from models (primary structure).
  - C: denature egg albumin with heat, 10% HNO₃, 10% NaOH, 95% ethanol and 1% AgNO₃, plus an untreated Control.
  - D: isolate casein from ~20 mL nonfat milk with 20% acetic acid at ~50 °C, then calculate % casein.
  - E: biuret and ninhydrin tests on glycine, tyrosine, gelatin, egg albumin and casein.
- **Central objective:** relate the levels of protein structure to peptide bonds, denaturation and the isoelectric point, and tell amino acids from proteins with color tests.
- **Reagents as stated:**
  - Part C: egg white in 200 mL water, 2–3 mL per tube; 10% HNO₃ 2 mL; 10% NaOH 2 mL; 95% ethanol 4 mL; 1% AgNO₃ 10 drops.
  - Part D: nonfat milk ~20 mL; 20% acetic acid dropwise (2–3 mL).
  - Part E samples: 1% glycine; tyrosine (saturated, or in dilute NaOH); 1% gelatin; 1% egg albumin; casein (spatula tip).
  - Part E reagents: 10% NaOH 2 mL; biuret reagent (5% CuSO₄) 5 drops; 0.2% ninhydrin 1 mL (**solvent not stated**).
- **Equipment:** model kits; test tubes (size not stated); 10- and 250-mL cylinders; cheesecloth; Bunsen burner or boiling bath; 150-mL beaker; hot plate or 50 °C bath; thermometer; funnel with filter paper or coffee filter (Büchner optional); watch glass; pH paper; balance.
- **Heating:**
  - C.1: Bunsen flame or boiling bath, 5 min.
  - D.3: milk at 50 °C.
  - E.2: boiling bath, 4–5 min.
  - There are no hood steps, no UV and no vacuum (the Büchner is optional).
- **Time (estimates):**
  - A: 15–20 min.
  - B: 20–30 min. The bottleneck is class coordination.
  - C: 25–30 min. It needs one shared egg-white prep.
  - D: 40–60 min. The bottleneck is gravity filtration of curds, and the casein will not be dry within the period.
  - E: 30 min, including the boiling bath.
  - Total ≈ 2.5–3 h, which is tight for a 3-h period.
- **Report outputs:**
  - A.1 and A.2 structures and the hydrolysis equation.
  - B.1 and B.2: amino acid, sequence, structure, equation.
  - C: observations and explanations for 6 tubes.
  - D: masses, two pH values, % casein.
  - E: a 6×5 color table, including the reagent-color row.
- **Pre-lab:** 7 questions. **Post-lab:** Q.1–Q.7.
- **Textbook topics:** amino acids, peptide bonds, protein structure levels, denaturation, isoelectric point (Exp 12 is the prerequisite).

## 4. Science and calculations

- **Figure 1** (p. 167): generic condensation is correct, with H₂O removed and the peptide bond boxed. **Figure 2** (p. 168): Gly–Ala, with H on Gly and CH₃ on Ala, hydrolysis to glycine + alanine. Correct.
- **Figure 3** (p. 169):
  - The (a) chain H₃N⁺ … COO⁻ is consistent with the caption.
  - (b) shows an α-helix and an antiparallel sheet with H-bonds.
  - (c) shows S–S, salt bridge, H-bond and hydrophobic interactions. The legend has both colors and text labels, so it does not rely on color alone.
  - (d) shows hemoglobin as α₂β₂. Correct.
- **Biuret statement** ("two or more peptide bonds… tripeptides and larger"): correct. The expected E.1 results are glycine and tyrosine negative (blue), and gelatin, albumin and casein positive (violet).
- **Ninhydrin:** the expected E.2 results are glycine positive (blue-violet) and tyrosine positive (may be weak, see above). The proteins gelatin and albumin are positive but weaker, and the casein solid is weak or uncertain.
- **Table 1** says heavy metals "Bond to –SH and –COO⁻ groups… which breaks disulfide bonds". This is imprecise: Ag⁺ and Hg²⁺ bind free thiols and carboxylates and disrupt salt bridges. Cleaving existing disulfides is not the main mechanism. Exp 14 states it correctly ("bind tightly to sulfur-containing (–SH) R groups"). P3 Science.
- **C.5 AgNO₃ confound (P1 Science):**
  - Egg white contains chloride (roughly 0.15–0.18 g Cl⁻ per 100 g; verify). One egg white (~33 g) has ≈ 50–60 mg Cl⁻. In ~233 mL that is ≈ 0.24 mg/mL, so 2.5 mL ≈ 0.6 mg Cl⁻ ≈ 0.017 mmol.
  - 10 drops of 1% AgNO₃ ≈ 0.5 mL × 10 mg/mL = 5 mg ÷ 169.87 g/mol = 0.029 mmol Ag⁺.
  - So roughly half or more of the Ag⁺ can precipitate as **white AgCl**, and more if tap water was used. That precipitate looks like coagulated protein. Students will "see denaturation" that is at least partly AgCl, and the manual never mentions this.
  - Needs a bench test, for example AgNO₃ added to a chloride-matched blank, or distilled water specified.
- **C.3 (10% NaOH):** dilute egg albumin + NaOH (final ~4–5% NaOH) often shows little or no visible precipitate; it may clear, turn slightly yellow, or gel slowly. Table 1 and the Goal ("Observe how… base… denature a protein") lead students to expect coagulation. They risk recording the expected result. Needs bench test and a sentence that a protein can be denatured without visibly precipitating.
- **D.3 isoelectric point wording (P2 Science):** "Measure the pH of the liquid. This is the pH at which casein becomes insoluble (its isoelectric point)." After dropwise excess acetic acid the measured whey pH is usually *below* the pI (casein pI ≈ 4.6). pH paper reads ±0.5. The value is an approximation of the pI, not its measurement.
- **Acid amount check (estimate):** milk needs ≈ 30–40 mmol/L of acid to reach pH 4.6, so 20 mL needs ≈ 0.6–0.8 mmol. 20% (w/v) acetic acid ≈ 3.3 M, and 2–3 mL ≈ 6.7–10 mmol, which is ~10× more. The "you may need 2–3 mL" is therefore high, but adding drop by drop and stopping when no more solid forms protects against gross overshoot. Needs bench test.
- **Pre-lab 7 recompute:** 0.62 g ÷ 20.45 g × 100% = 3.0317% → **3.0%** (2 significant figures, limited by 0.62 g). This is plausible for *dry* casein; skim milk is ≈ 2.5–2.8% casein. Students' wet-casein values will be higher (see D.4).
- **Milk mass check:** 20 mL × 1.035 g/mL ≈ 20.7 g, consistent with 20.45 g in the pre-lab.
- **Copying risk:** Table 2 and the Intro give every expected color, and the Goals and Table 1 imply that every agent coagulates the protein. With no observation prompts beyond "describe any changes", students may record expected rather than observed results, especially for C.3, C.5 and the weak ninhydrin tests.

Defects found:

- **Amylase concentration and source are never stated** (Materials, Instructor Notes). Part A's discrimination (3 vs 6 vs 10 drops within 10 min) depends entirely on that concentration.
- **Ceiling effect in B, C, D (P1):** Part A shows that 3–10 drops (~0.15–0.5 mL) are enough to see hydrolysis in 10 min. Parts B, C and D use **4 mL** of the same amylase, which is 8–27× the Part A maximum, and run for 10–15 min. Even suboptimal conditions (ice, pH 4, maybe pH 10, ethanol) can reach level 3 (yellow-brown) within the time, so no optimum can be seen. Goal 4 ("find the optimum temperature and pH") may then be unobservable. This needs a bench test with the actual enzyme; possible fixes are diluting the enzyme for B–D or sampling at several times, but those are co-author decisions.
- **C.1–C.2 buffered starch (P1):**
  - The A Materials list "1% starch (buffered at pH 7.0)", and Getting Ready dispenses one beaker of 1% starch for all parts. Part C therefore mixes 4 mL pH-2 (or pH-10) buffer + 4 mL amylase + 4 mL **pH-7-buffered** starch.
  - The final pH is not 2, 4, 7 or 10 unless the test buffers are much stronger than the starch buffer. There is no step to measure the final pH and no report field for it, yet the graph x-axis uses the nominal values.
  - Needs a stockroom/bench check; a "measured pH of mixture" row would fix the data.
- **Part D has no no-additive control.** NaCl is described as an activator ("actually help salivary amylase work"), so it is not a neutral comparison. Students answer "Inhibitor? (yes/no)" without a baseline.
- **D.1 Ag⁺ in buffered media:** if the buffered starch or the amylase stock contains Cl⁻ or phosphate, Ag⁺ precipitates as AgCl or Ag₃PO₄ and inhibition may be partial. Ag⁺ added is 0.029 mmol (10 drops × 0.05 mL × 10 mg/mL ÷ 169.87 g/mol), ≈ 3.4 mM in 8.5 mL. Needs a stockroom answer on the buffer composition.
- **D.3 and the iodine test:** residual Ag⁺ in the spot well forms a little yellow AgI with the KI in the reagent. That could be read as "yellow-brown" (level 3 = no inhibition). Needs bench test.
- **A.3 Benedict's:** commercial enzyme preparations are often formulated with maltodextrin or sugar carriers. If so, tubes 2–4 may turn Benedict's-positive without any hydrolysis. There is no enzyme-only blank. Needs a stockroom/SDS check.
- **Test tube size:** "6 test tubes" in A, B and D, with no size. A.3 reaches ~7.5 mL (4 mL + up to 10 drops + 3 mL Benedict's) and is then heated in boiling water; B and D reach 8 mL. In a 13 × 100 mm tube (~8–9 mL) this overfills or splashes when heated.

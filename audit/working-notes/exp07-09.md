# Experiment 7: Amines and Amides

## 1. Inventory
- **Parts:**
  - A: classify amines with models (ammonia, methylamine, dimethylamine, trimethylamine, aniline).
  - B: odor, water solubility, and pH of aniline, *N*-methylaniline, and triethylamine (tubes 1–3, in the hood).
  - C: add 10% HCl to the same tubes to form amine salts; observe solubility and odor; write equations.
  - D: amide models (D.1, D.3); acetamide and benzamide odor and solubility (D.2); acid hydrolysis with 10% HCl (D.4, tubes 1 and 3); base hydrolysis with 10% NaOH (D.5, tubes 2 and 4), detected with moist red litmus held above the tube.
- **Central objective:** amines are bases and form odorless, soluble salts; amides are neutral; amide hydrolysis gives different products in acid and in base.

- **Time (estimate):**
  - A: 20 min
  - B: 20 min
  - C: 15 min
  - D: 45 min
  - Total: about 1 h 45 min to 2 h.
  - Bottleneck: the single 250-mL boiling bath in the hood, if it is shared by the class (F7-03).

  - A.1/A.2 table

  - C table (solubility and odor after HCl)

**Volumes:** Each D tube holds about 4 mL. If 13 × 100 mm tubes are used (capacity about 10 mL), there is enough headspace. Tube size is not stated anywhere.

Defects:
- **C.1 (line 78): the endpoint can arrive before the aromatic amines dissolve (P1).**
  - Quote: "Add 10% HCl … until a drop of the solution on the stirring rod turns blue litmus paper red. Record any change in the solubility."
  - The conjugate acids of aniline (pKa 4.6) and *N*-methylaniline (pKa 4.85) are inside litmus's acid range (red below about pH 4.5–5).
  - While undissolved amine remains, the solution is buffered at pH ≈ pKa + log([B]sat/[BH⁺]).
  - Calculation for *N*-methylaniline:
    - 5 drops ≈ 0.25 mL × 0.989 g/mL = 0.247 g; ÷ 107.15 g/mol = 2.31 mmol. This needs about 16 drops of 10% HCl (0.144 mmol per drop).
    - Aqueous solubility is about 0.56 g/100 mL, so the saturated free base is about 0.052 M.
    - pH 4.5 is reached when [BH⁺] ≈ 0.052 × 10^0.35 = 0.12 M. In about 2.3 mL that is 0.27 mmol, or about 2 drops.
    - So blue litmus can turn red after about 2 of the 16 drops needed, with about 85–90% of the *N*-methylaniline still an oily layer.
  - Calculation for aniline:
    - 2.74 mmol needs about 19 drops.
    - With saturated free base 0.385 M, pH 4.5 is reached at about 1.1 mmol HCl (about 8 drops). Roughly a quarter of the aniline is still undissolved at that point.
  - Result: students who follow the instruction may record "no change" for the aromatic amines. That contradicts the intro, Pre-lab Q4, and Q.4.
  - Needs a bench test. Proposed for co-author review: "until the oily layer or cloudiness disappears (about 15–20 drops), then confirm with blue litmus."
- **Instructor note (line 65) vs hazard (line 53) (P1, safety).**
  - Instructor note: "start a boiling water bath in the fume hood … on a hot plate … Place the dropper bottles of aniline, *N*-methylaniline, and triethylamine in the fume hood."
  - Hazard: triethylamine is "highly flammable … keep it away from hot plates." (Flash point about −11 °C; verify on SDS.)
  - The manual puts both in the same hood and never tells anyone to keep them apart or to use a separate hood.
- **Bath capacity (line 65, line 81) (P2).** One "250-mL beaker about half full" bath is described in the singular. Twelve groups × 4 tubes = 48 tubes cannot run in one 250-mL beaker. It is unclear whether there is one bath per group or per class. If per class, D.4/D.5 becomes the bottleneck.
- **D.5 has no negative control (P2).** No tube of 2 mL water + 2 mL 10% NaOH, heated the same way, shows that the litmus stays red without amide. Bumping NaOH spray could also turn the paper blue. The intro warns about this only for touching. A control would make the result interpretable. Needs a bench test.
- **D.4 acid hydrolysis is time-limited (P2).**
  - 2 mL water + 2 mL 10% HCl gives about 1.4 M HCl; 5 min in a boiling water bath (about 95 °C).
  - Acetamide acid hydrolysis is slow (half-life roughly tens of minutes under these conditions). The vinegar odor may be faint or absent, and wafting inside a hood with the sash down limits odor detection.
  - Pre-lab Q6 and Figure 5 tell students to expect vinegar, so the risk is that they record the expected result. Needs a bench test.
  - No guidance is given if no odor is detected.
- **D.4/D.5: tube size not stated (P3).**
- **D.2 (line 83) (P3).** "Cautiously note the odor of each amide by wafting" happens before water is added, so the amides are dry solids. This is fine, but acetamide's "mousy" odor (from impurities) may confuse students who expect "little or no odor".
- **Report, C table (P3).** No field for the number of HCl drops. That number would let students and instructors see the C.1 endpoint problem.

## 3. Bench executability (dry run)

**Tube and vessel tracking:**
- A: 1–2 fermentation tubes per group.
  - The unknown's fermentation tube is not assigned. A.1 says each group gets "1 or 2 fermentation tubes … for the entire class", but each group has its *own* unknown. D.3 needs the unknown's fermentation result, yet nothing tells each group to set up a tube for its unknown (P2).
- B: 7 tubes, each 10 drops sample + 2 mL Benedict's.
- C: 7 tubes, each 10 drops sample + 2 mL Seliwanoff's.
- D: 6 wells.
- E: 4 tubes, each 3 mL sample + 20 drops (1 mL) HCl or water.
  - The HCl tubes then get about 1.0–1.25 mL NaOH, so they hold about 5.2 mL.
  - E.3 removes 5 drops; E.4 adds 2 mL Benedict's, giving about 7 mL.
  - In a 13 × 100 mm tube (about 10 mL), 7 mL in a boiling bath leaves little headspace (bumping risk). Tube size is not stated (P2).
- F: 3 tubes, each with food + 2 mL water.

Defects:
- **A.2 (line 79) (P2, Editorial).** "Fill the rest of the tube until … the open bulb is about ¾ full (Figure 2)". The fermentation tube is **Figure 1**; Figure 2 is the maltose/sucrose Haworth figure. This is a wrong cross-reference.
- **Part A has no negative control (P2, Science).** There is no yeast + water tube, although B and C have water blanks. Yeast can release some CO₂ from its own reserves, and a "small gas pocket" (A.3 categories) cannot be interpreted without a blank. Needs a bench test.
- **Unknown set never defined (P1, Science).**
  - Table 1 lists galactose and maltose as "Carbohydrates Used in This Experiment", but no step tests them; they appear only in Q.8.
  - If the unknowns can include maltose or galactose, D.3 cannot work:
    - maltose has the same A–D pattern as glucose (+ fermentation, + Benedict's, slow Seliwanoff's, – iodine);
    - galactose has the same pattern as lactose (– fermentation, + Benedict's, slow, –).
  - The manual never says which carbohydrates can be unknowns. Add an instructor note limiting unknowns to the five reference solutions, or add maltose and galactose to Parts A–D.
- **E.2 → E.3 order biases the starch result (P1, Science).**
  - The intro (line 56) states that "excess base also destroys the iodine color and gives a false negative iodine test."
  - E.2 neutralizes the HCl tubes until red litmus turns **blue**, which means pH above about 8, already basic. Only then does E.3 run the iodine test.
  - The water-control starch tube receives no NaOH.
  - So any fading in the starch + HCl well can come from base rather than hydrolysis, and the bias is toward the expected answer.
  - Ten minutes in about 0.7 M HCl at about 95 °C may also hydrolyze starch only partly (dextrins still give a blue/purple color). Needs a bench test.
  - Proposed for co-author review: run E.3 on the cooled, still-acidic samples *before* E.2 (iodine works in acid), and neutralize only for E.4.
- **F.3 (line 107) (P2, Science).**
  - Food + 2 mL water + 2 mL Seliwanoff's halves the HCl concentration (to about 1.5–3 M) compared with Part C (10 drops + 2 mL), yet it is read at the same 1-minute cut-off calibrated in Part C.
  - Fructose- or sucrose-containing foods may read "slow" or "negative" (false negative).
  - Highly concentrated glucose foods (corn syrup) may redden faster than the 2% glucose reference.
  - Needs a bench test. Report F has no 5-minute reading.
- **C.2 (line 90) (P3).** "Place all of the test tubes in the boiling water bath at the same time": 7 tubes in a half-full 400-mL beaker need a rack or holder in the bath, which is not listed. Reading 7 colors at exactly 1 min is hard. Suggest staggering the tubes or reading against a white background (bench test).
- **E.1 (line 99) (P3, Editorial).** The tubes are labeled after the reagents are added ("Label the four tubes and heat them"). The label step should come first.
- **Part D materials (line 93) (P3).** The note about bottles with pipets ("dropper bottles are not used because the solutions grow mold") appears only in Part D, but Parts A–C use the same solutions.

## 4. Science, calculations, and test specificity

### Equations and numbers
- **Fermentation:** C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ is balanced.
- **Benedict's:** R–CHO + 2 Cu²⁺ + 5 OH⁻ → R–COO⁻ + Cu₂O + 3 H₂O.
  - O: 1 + 5 = 6 on the left; 2 + 1 + 3 = 6 on the right.
  - H: 1 + 5 = 6 on the left; 6 on the right.
  - Charge: +4 − 5 = −1 on the left; −1 on the right.
  - Electrons: aldehyde C goes from +1 to +3 (2 e⁻); 2 Cu²⁺ → 2 Cu⁺ (2 e⁻).
  - **Balanced.**
- **E.2 NaOH volume:**
  - 10% HCl = 104.7 g/L × 0.10 ÷ 36.46 = 2.87 M; 20 drops ≈ 1.0 mL = 2.87 mmol.
  - 10% NaOH = 110.9 g/L × 0.10 ÷ 40.00 = 2.77 M.
  - 2.87 ÷ 2.77 = 1.04 mL ≈ 21 drops.
  - The manual's "about 20–25 drops" is **consistent**.
- **E.1 HCl in the tube:** 2.87 mmol ÷ 4 mL ≈ 0.72 M. Sucrose hydrolysis should be extensive in 10 min at about 95 °C. Starch hydrolysis is probably partial (bench test).

### Figures
- Fig 1 (fig8_4): fermentation tube, OK. Minor issue: in the positive tube the liquid level in the bulb is drawn at the same height as in the negative tube. Displaced liquid should raise the bulb level (P3).
- Fig 2 (fig8_2): correct (see Exp 8).
- Fig 3 (fig8_3): ketose ⇌ enediol ⇌ aldose is correct. The parenthetical "glucose or mannose" is correct.

### Is any test presented as perfectly specific? (brief item)
- **Fermentation:** hedged appropriately (galactose "very slowly or not at all", maltose "may start more slowly"). Acceptable. The missing yeast blank (above) is the real gap.
- **Benedict's:**
  - Table 2 "What it detects: Reducing sugars", and F (line 58) "a positive Benedict's test shows reducing sugars (such as glucose …)".
  - Neither says that Benedict's also responds to other reducing substances, such as ascorbic acid in fruit juice. Fruit juice is one of the listed foods. Benedict's is also only semi-quantitative.
  - **P2:** add one hedge sentence (F9-12).
- **Seliwanoff's:**
  - Handled well: "Ketoses react much faster than aldoses", aldoses give "light pink only after several minutes", sucrose also reacts, and results are recorded as fast/slow/none.
  - Remaining problems:
    - The positive-result wording in C.2 ("A deep red color that forms this quickly shows a ketose") does not mention sucrose.
    - The cut-off depends on concentration and on the HCl strength (see F.3).
- **Iodine:**
  - "Amylose (starch) and other polysaccharides with coiled chains" is acceptable. Cellulose is explained as negative.
  - The colors "red-brown" (positive for glycogen and amylopectin) and "yellow-brown" (negative) are hard to tell apart visually. No reference well (water + iodine) is included in D.1, and Water is marked "(not tested)" in the report for D.2 (P3).

### Other science points
- **Starch in Benedict's:** "little or no color". Commercial soluble starch can contain reducing dextrins and give a faint green. Students then may call it "+" and confuse the unknown ID. Needs a bench test.
- **Copying risk:**
  - The intro explicitly gives each sugar's fermentation result (line 36), and Table 2 gives every positive result.
  - The A–D table asks for a color and a ±, which is good. But nothing asks students to note when their observation differs from the expected result. An optional prompt in Q.1–Q.4 would help (P3).
